# Write and edit articles

Help the intended reader get the main answer from the opening, understand the reasons by scanning the headings, and find the evidence and implementation details when needed.

## Establish the brief

- Identify the audience, its main question, the requested deliverable, and the available evidence. Carry forward constraints on language, voice, length, and format. Ask only when the missing information materially changes the thesis or the deliverable.
- For an outline, give the thesis, claim-based headings, and the evidence needed under each. For a draft, deliver the article. For a review, identify concrete structural problems and give suggested changes. Apply a rewrite when requested.
- When revising, preserve the author's position, facts, attribution, and voice. Flag unsupported claims and gaps that affect the conclusion. Narrow or qualify claims to match the evidence. Never invent a fact, quote, source, test result, or consensus.

## Organize the argument with the Minto Pyramid Principle

1. Lead with a specific answer to the reader's main question. Use a title that identifies the subject and a lead that states the takeaway and any condition that could change the reader's decision. For a conditional recommendation, state the condition in the same paragraph as the recommendation, even if you explain how to check it later. Where context is necessary, use a brief situation and complication to make the question clear, then answer it in the opening.
2. Group the reasons, findings, or steps that support the answer. Give each section one distinct purpose. Together the sections should cover the argument at the promised scope. Order them by the logic of the piece: importance for recommendations, sequence for procedures, and dependency for explanations.
3. Make each section's heading or first sentence express its point. Put the evidence directly under the claim it supports. Keep reasons, supporting evidence, and implications distinct. Reflect material counterevidence in the claim itself or alongside it.

## Layer the detail through progressive disclosure

- **Opening:** the takeaway, why it matters, and the essential scope or limiting condition. A reader who stops here should understand what the article establishes and when it applies.
- **Main text:** the reasoning, evidence needed to assess it, and examples or steps needed to follow it. Use descriptive headings and format comparisons or ordered steps for scanning.
- **Further detail:** optional derivations, extended examples, edge cases, raw data, reference configurations, and methodology that readers can skip without losing the main argument. Use labeled subsections, an appendix, or descriptive links. Use collapsible content only when the format supports it and the content is genuinely optional.
- Keep prerequisites, material limitations, and anything needed for a safe or correct decision visible where they first matter. Scale the layers to the length and genre. A short article can layer information within a few paragraphs.

## Check the reader's path

- Read only the title and opening. The answer and its scope should be clear.
- Scan the headings and first sentences. Each point should support the main answer, and the logical order should be apparent.
- Read the evidence for each material claim. Check source links, factual scope, quotations, numbers, and what the evidence can establish. Use accessible primary sources where appropriate. If evidence is missing, qualify the claim or identify the gap in the requested format.
- Check where readers encounter caveats and prerequisites. They should appear before the reader could misinterpret the conclusion or act on incomplete information.
